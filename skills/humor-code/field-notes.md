# Field notes

Where each idea in `SKILL.md` came from. Nine chapters, nine questions, one road trip. Peter McGraw is the scientist, Joel Warner the journalist. Warner bets that the research will make McGraw funnier. The book ends with the test.

## 1. Colorado — what makes things funny?

The setup. McGraw runs the Humor Research Lab (HuRL) at the Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder.

The old theories and why each fails on its own:

- **Superiority** (Plato, Hobbes): we laugh at other people's flaws. Cannot explain puns or tickling.
- **Relief** (Freud): laughter vents blocked nervous energy. Untestable and mostly wrong.
- **Incongruity**: we laugh when expectation breaks. Too broad. Most surprises are not funny, and a tickle is funny without any surprise.

McGraw builds on Thomas Veatch's 1998 paper, which required a norm violation and a simultaneous sense that things are normal. McGraw's version is the benign violation: violation, benign, at the same time.

The lab tests it on ugly cases. A church raffles a Hummer to recruit members: rated funnier by people who found it wrong *and* okay. A man rubs a dead kitten against his face: funnier at a distance. The pattern holds where other theories break.

McGraw's own first stand-up set, at the Squire Lounge open mic in Denver, dies. That is the baseline.

E.B. White's warning opens the book: analyse humor like a frog and the frog dies. The book's counter-argument is that the frog is already dead and someone should look inside.

## 2. Los Angeles — who is funny?

Comedy as a job, not a personality. They shadow working comics and the industry around them.

- No stable "funny person" trait predicts a career. Output does.
- Comics are not sadder than average as a group, despite the myth. The correlation people quote is weak.
- Every good bit was rebuilt on stage over months. Comics carry notebooks and treat each set as an experiment with one variable changed.
- Louis C.K.'s method by reputation: throw out the whole hour every year and start again. Volume forces new material.

Takeaway: the stage is the instrument. You cannot write funny alone in a room and then deliver it.

## 3. New York — how do you make funny?

The Onion writers' room. The process:

- Writers bring roughly 600 headlines a week. About 16 make it.
- Headline first. If the headline is not funny the story cannot save it.
- The room kills almost everything, out loud, fast. No defending your idea.
- The house voice is a fixed frame: a straight news register applied to a violation. The register itself is the benign half, which is why the paper can print unbearably dark headlines.

Takeaway: quantity, then a merciless filter. And a strong fixed frame lets you go much darker than you otherwise could.

## 4. Tanzania — why do we laugh?

The 1962 Tanganyika laughter epidemic. It began with three girls at a mission-run girls' school in Kashasha, near Bukoba. It spread until the school closed, then jumped to nearby villages and ran for months, affecting around a thousand people. Nobody found anything funny. It was mass psychogenic contagion under stress.

Robert Provine's laughter research is the other half of the chapter:

- People laugh around 30 times more in company than alone.
- Fewer than one in five laughs follows anything that looks like a joke.
- The speaker laughs more than the audience.
- Laughter punctuates speech at phrase boundaries. It never interrupts a clause.
- You cannot laugh convincingly on demand, and it is hard to stop.

Conclusion: laughter evolved before jokes, as a play signal in rough-and-tumble play. It means "this is safe". Jokes are a later use of an older tool. Chimps and rats make the same signal.

Takeaway: laughter measures the social frame, not the quality of the material.

## 5. Japan — when is comedy lost in translation?

Manzai, the two-hander from Osaka: the boke is the fool who says the wrong thing, the tsukkomi corrects him and smacks him. The roles are fixed before a word is spoken, so the audience knows in advance that everything is play. The form supplies the benign half.

Yoshimoto Kogyo runs comedy in Japan as an industry with a school and a pipeline, closer to a talent factory than a scene.

Tighter norms mean more things count as violations. So Japanese comedy leans on strong, permanent, explicit frames: fixed roles, sanctioned settings, designated fools.

Takeaway: structure travels, content does not. If you cannot share your audience's norms, declare the frame out loud.

## 6. Scandinavia — does humor have a dark side?

The Danish Muhammad cartoons, published by *Jyllands-Posten* in 2005, and what followed: embassies burned, people killed, cartoonist Kurt Westergaard under permanent guard. They interview editor Flemming Rose and Westergaard.

The theory's reading: for the readers the paper had in mind, the drawings were a benign violation about press freedom. For readers who hold the norm hard, there was no benign route at all. Same object, two audiences, and one of them was only attacked.

Two more ideas from the chapter:

- Humor comes with deniability. *I was joking* is an escape hatch, which is exactly what makes humor useful for saying the unsayable and equally useful for cruelty.
- Denmark's own self-image as a place of tolerant irony did not protect anyone. Nobody's sense of humor is the neutral one.

Takeaway: ask where the violation lands and whether that person has a route to "this is okay". If not, you have made an attack with a joke's alibi.

## 7. Palestine — can you find humor where you least expect it?

Ramallah, Jerusalem, and a comedy tour through the West Bank. They find far more comedy than expected, and the reason follows from the theory: in a place with endless violation, what is scarce is the benign frame, and a joke manufactures one.

- Checkpoint jokes, occupation jokes, and jokes about their own leaders are common and unsentimental.
- The comedy is not escape. It is a claim of proportion: I am bigger than this thing.
- Outsiders who arrive to "bring laughter" mostly misjudge the frame. Locals set it correctly because they know the norms.

Takeaway: gallows humor is a control move. Hardship raises the supply of violations, so humor rises with it rather than falling.

## 8. The Amazon — is laughter the best medicine?

They join Patch Adams and a boatload of volunteer clowns down the Peruvian Amazon, and visit the laughter yoga world.

Findings, stated honestly by the book:

- The direct medical evidence for laughter is thin. Studies are small, poorly controlled, and often measure mood for an hour.
- Norman Cousins' famous self-cure story is an anecdote, and he was on other treatment.
- Laughter yoga produces laughter with no joke at all, which supports the play-signal account of laughter and undercuts the joke-based one.
- What plausibly helps is what the clowning delivers by accident: attention, contact, and a sense that the situation is not entirely in charge of you.

Takeaway: do not sell laughter as a cure. Sell it as company and as control.

## 9. Montreal — punch line

Just for Laughs, the largest comedy festival, where the industry buys and sells. The gatekeepers, the showcases, the agents.

McGraw performs. He is better than Denver and not good. The wager resolves as a qualified yes: study made him funnier, and did not make him a comedian.

The closing position:

- Humor is a mechanism. You can reverse-engineer it, and the analysis does not kill it.
- The mechanism is one thing pointed two ways. What lets a joke bond people is the same thing that lets it wound them.
- Being funny is a practice with a known method: find the violation, build the benign frame, repeat in front of real people, cut what dies.
