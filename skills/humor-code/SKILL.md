# The humor code

One mechanism explains every laugh. Learn it and you can build a joke on purpose, and repair a broken one instead of throwing it away.

Source: *The Humor Code: A Global Search for What Makes Things Funny*, Peter McGraw and Joel Warner, 2014. McGraw runs the Humor Research Lab (HuRL) at the University of Colorado Boulder. The theory extends Thomas Veatch's 1998 work.

## The one rule

**Something is funny when it is a violation and benign at the same time.**

Three conditions, all required:

1. **Violation.** It threatens how you think the world should be. Wrong, unsettling, or unsafe.
2. **Benign.** It is also okay, safe, or acceptable.
3. **At the same time.** Not one, then the other.

Every failure is one of these three:

| What went wrong | What you get | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violation, not benign | offensive, sad, disturbing | add distance or a safe frame |
| Benign, no violation | boring, pleasant, nice | break something harder |
| Both, but in sequence | confusion, then relief | compress it, cut the setup |

So the craft is two moves. Find the violation. Find the frame that makes it benign. Weak material almost always skips one of them.

## What counts as a violation

Anything that breaks a rule, not just rude things:

- physical: a tickle, a pratfall, a play fight
- dignity: an insult, mockery, self-mockery
- social: manners, taboos, who may say what
- moral: cheating, cruelty, sacrilege
- logic: absurdity, nonsense, impossible scale
- language: puns, wrong register, a mangled word
- expectation: misdirection, a broken pattern
- context: sarcasm, irony, the right words in the wrong room

This is why incongruity alone fails as a theory. A tickle is funny and not incongruous. A corpse in the kitchen is incongruous and not funny.

## The three levers that make a violation benign

Pick one. Pick a second if the first is not enough.

1. **Psychological distance.** Push it away in time, space, social relation, or reality. "Long ago", "far away", "to someone not like you", "it never happened".
2. **An alternative norm.** A second reading where the thing is fine. Puns run on this: one meaning breaks the sentence, another saves it. So do frames like "we are playing", "this is a comedy club", "I am the fool here".
3. **Weak commitment to the norm.** The audience does not much care about the rule you broke. You cannot change this in the moment, so you choose the room or you choose the rule.

## The sweet spot

Distance is a curve, not a line. Too close is tragedy. Too far is boring. The window sits between.

McGraw, Williams and Warren, *The Rise and Fall of Humor* (2014), tested jokes from the @AHurricaneSandy account on 1,064 people over 100 days. Ratings rose, peaked 36 days after the storm, then fell. Comedy is not tragedy plus time. It is tragedy plus the right distance, and the window shuts again.

**Match the dial to the size of the violation.** A small mishap is funniest up close: your stubbed toe, now. A large one is funniest far off: a stranger, long ago, hypothetically. Severe and close is horror. Mild and distant is nothing at all.

The four dials:

- time: too soon, or stale
- space: down the road, or another continent
- social: me and mine, or a stranger
- reality: it happened, or it is a cartoon

When a joke lands wrong, do not bin it. Slide it along one dial.

## Funny is local

Benign is a fact about the audience, not about the joke. Same violation, different room, different result. There is no universal joke. There is a universal mechanism.

Before you write, name the audience and name the norms they hold hard. Those norms are the ones that hurt when broken, and the ones that pay best when you can frame them safe.

## Laughter is mostly not about jokes

Robert Provine recorded laughter in the wild. Findings:

- People laugh about 30 times more with company than alone.
- Fewer than one in five laughs follows anything joke-shaped. "I'll see you later" gets laughs.
- The speaker laughs more than the listener.
- Laughter lands at phrase boundaries, never mid-clause. Speech outranks it.

Read it this way: laughter is a play signal older than jokes. It says *this is safe, we are playing*. That is why it spreads. In 1962 a laughter epidemic began with three girls at a school in Kashasha, Tanganyika, closed the school, and ran through the district for months. Nobody involved found anything funny. It was contagion.

**Practical effect: build the play frame before you bring material.** The frame does more work than the lines. A crowd that already knows it is allowed to laugh will laugh at far less.

## Funny is a practice, not a gift

- **The Onion** writers produce roughly 600 headlines a week. About 16 survive. Headline first, story second. Volume plus a brutal room, not inspiration.
- **Stand-ups write on stage.** The set is the lab. A joke moves from dead to strong on a one-word change you cannot predict from the page.
- **Nobody starts funny.** McGraw bombed at a Denver open mic at the start of the book, and was merely acceptable at Just for Laughs at the end, after a year of study and practice. The gap is repetition, not talent.

Craft moves that follow from the theory:

- Put the violation last. The funny word ends the sentence.
- Take the shortest path to it. Every extra word delays the two perceptions and breaks simultaneity.
- Be specific. "A dog" is nothing. "A wet Alsatian" is a picture. Concrete detail makes the violation real enough to matter.
- Tag it. After a punchline lands, hit the same violation again from a new angle. The frame is already built, so tags are cheap.
- Call back. A callback is a violation the room has already agreed is benign.
- Act it out. Show the thing instead of reporting it, and you shorten the path.
- Cut the setup to the bone. Setup only exists to establish the norm you will break.

## The dark side

The Danish cartoon crisis is the book's hard case. A violation with no benign route for one audience is not a joke to them. It is an attack. They are not humourless. They hold the norm.

Humor carries a built-in escape hatch: *I was joking*. That is what lets it say the unsayable, and it is also what makes it a clean weapon. Ask where the violation lands. A joke aimed at power is easy to frame benign, because the target can absorb it. A joke aimed at someone who cannot is a violation with the benign half missing. That is the whole of "punch up, not down", stated mechanically.

## Humor where life is hardest

In the West Bank the book found more comedy, not less. The reason follows from the theory. Violation is everywhere, so the scarce half is the benign frame, and a joke supplies it. A joke about the checkpoint is a claim that you are larger than the checkpoint. Gallows humor is a control move, not denial.

## Humor across cultures

Japan shows that structure travels and content does not. Manzai fixes the roles in advance: the boke says the stupid thing, the tsukkomi corrects him and hits him. The form declares the violation safe before it happens. Tight social norms make more things violations, so the culture builds strong permanent frames to let those violations be funny.

When you cannot share an audience's norms, do the same. Name the game out loud.

## Is laughter medicine?

Mostly no, and the book says so. Patch Adams' clown trip down the Amazon and the laughter yoga scene both rest on thin evidence. The direct physical effect of laughter is small and poorly supported.

What does hold up is company and control. Laughter marks a safe group and a threat you have got the measure of. Sell it as connection, never as a cure.

## At work

McGraw is a marketing professor, and the applied lesson is narrow. Humor buys attention, memory, and liking. It also discounts your message and can cost you credibility on a serious subject. If the violation has nothing to do with the product, the audience keeps the joke and forgets you. Tie the violation to the thing you are selling, or do not use it.

## How to use this

To write something funny:

1. Name the audience and the norms they hold.
2. Pick the norm to break. Break it harder than feels comfortable.
3. Pick the benign lever: distance, an alternative norm, or a norm they barely hold.
4. Compress until both halves arrive together.
5. Test on the real audience. Your own read is worthless.

To fix something that died, ask in this order:

1. Was there any violation at all? Most flat lines are merely pleasant.
2. Was there a route to benign? If not, add one dial of distance.
3. Did both halves arrive at once? If not, cut words before the punchline.
4. Wrong room? The same line works elsewhere.

To judge whether a joke goes too far: it goes too far when the audience holding the norm has no route to benign. That is a question about them, not about the words.
