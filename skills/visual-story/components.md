# The seven components in detail

Everything you can see on a screen is one of these. Each carries the same principle: contrast raises intensity, affinity lowers it.

## 1. Space

Space is the component all the others live inside. Four kinds.

**Deep space** stresses three dimensions. It is built from depth cues, and you want several at once:

- convergence, or linear perspective: parallel lines meet at a vanishing point
- size difference: the same object smaller further away
- overlap: a near object hides part of a far one, the strongest cue of all
- textural diffusion: texture gets finer and softer with distance
- aerial diffusion: haze, smoke, rain, dust washing out the far plane
- shape change: a circle seen at an angle is an ellipse
- tonal separation: light subject against dark background, or the reverse
- colour separation: warm advances, cool recedes
- up and down position: higher in frame reads as further away
- movement parallax: near objects sweep past faster than far ones
- focus: sharp near, soft far

**Flat space** stresses two dimensions. Build it by removing the cues: frontal planes square to the lens, size constancy, no overlap, even texture, long lens compression, and a background at the same tone and hue as the subject.

**Limited space** is flat planes stacked at separate depths, like cut-out layers. Frontal, but with clear intervals between them.

**Ambiguous space** gives no reference for where you are or how big anything is: extreme close-ups, darkness, fog, water, out of focus, blank fields. It collapses the moment a known object such as a hand or a face enters and gives back the scale.

Also in space:

- **The frame and the picture plane.** The flat surface everything is composed on. Aspect ratio is a space decision.
- **Open frame**: the world continues past the edges, characters move in and out, the frame feels like a window. **Closed frame**: everything needed is inside, composed and contained, the frame feels like a box. Closed is more controlled and more claustrophobic.
- **Surface divisions.** How many pieces the frame is cut into by edges, verticals, and lines. Few divisions is affinity. Many is contrast and business.
- **Lens.** Long flattens and compresses depth. Wide deepens it and exaggerates near against far. The single strongest space control on a set.

## 2. Line

Line appears wherever there is tonal contrast. It comes from three places:

- **contour**: the edge of an object
- **closure**: the implied line the eye draws between separate objects
- **axis**: the direction an object points, a look, an arm, a road

Three qualities to set:

- **Orientation**: horizontal, vertical, diagonal. Horizontal is the calmest, diagonal the most intense.
- **Direction**: the track a line or a moving object travels — left, right, up, down.
- **Quality**: straight through to fully curved. Straight is hard, curved is soft.

**The linear motif** is the dominant line of a frame, a scene, or a whole film. Reduce any frame to three or four lines and you have its motif. *Klute* moves its motif from horizontal to vertical to diagonal as the conflict tightens. *Matchpoint* starts on calm vertical affinity and ends on contrasting diagonals.

## 3. Shape

Everything reduces to three shapes in two dimensions — **circle, square, triangle** — and three in three dimensions — **sphere, cube, pyramid**.

Shape is made by line and by tonal contrast. Repeating one shape is affinity. Mixing them is contrast. Triangles carry the most intensity because they point.

## 4. Tone

Tone is brightness, the grey scale, independent of colour. It is the strongest attention control after movement: **the eye goes to the brightest part of the frame.**

Three ways to control it:

- **art direction**: the reflectance of the objects, lit evenly. The set decides the tones.
- **lighting**: light or hide selected parts. The lamp decides the tones.
- **exposure**: shift the whole range at once.

**Coincidence of tone** means the tonal edges line up with the object's edges, so the object reads clearly. **Non-coincidence** means light and shadow cut across the object, breaking it up and hiding it — shafts, blinds, dappled leaves, a face half in shadow. Non-coincidence is far more intense and is how you hide something in plain sight.

**Key**: high key is bright with a narrow range and low contrast. Low key is dark, with small bright areas and a wide range. Low key is the more intense.

## 5. Colour

Two systems: **additive** for light, which is screens and lamps, and **subtractive** for pigment, which is paint, dye, and set dressing.

Every colour is three numbers:

- **hue**: position on the wheel
- **saturation**: how vivid, from grey to pure
- **brightness**: how light or dark

Contrast comes from opposed hues, from warm against cool, and above all from **saturation difference**. One saturated object in a desaturated frame owns the shot.

Warm colours advance towards the viewer. Cool colours recede. That makes colour a depth cue as well as an emotional one.

**Colour has no fixed meaning.** It means what your exposition taught it to mean, and nothing else. Assign a hue to a character, a mood, or an idea in an early scene, hold it, and the audience will read it for the rest of the film without being told.

A **colour script** is the palette laid out shot by shot across the whole piece. Make one whenever the word "warm" could be argued about.

## 6. Movement

Movement is always **relative**: an object only moves against something that does not.

**Object movement** has a track, which is direction, plus quality and speed. Compound movement is two tracks at once, such as a person walking inside a moving train.

**Camera movement** splits in two:

- **2D moves**: pan, tilt, zoom, roll. Everything in frame shifts at the same rate. No new depth information.
- **3D moves**: dolly, track, crane, handheld. The camera changes position, so near objects slide past faster than far ones. Parallax appears, and parallax is the strongest depth cue there is. This is why a slow dolly makes a shot feel like film and a zoom does not.

**The continuum of movement.** Motion along the lens axis, towards or away from the camera, is the most intense. Motion across the frame is the least. Diagonal sits between. The same continuum applies to camera moves.

**Point of attention movement** is the eye travelling around a still frame. Its priority order is movement, then brightness, then faces, then the vanishing point, then anything newly entering.

## 7. Rhythm

Rhythm needs three things at once: **alternation**, **repetition**, and **tempo**.

It comes from three sources:

- **stationary objects**: repeated shapes across the frame, railings, windows, columns
- **moving objects**: anything that repeats a beat as it moves
- **editing**: each cut is a beat, and cut length is tempo

Motion only produces a beat when something *changes*: a change of speed, a change of direction, passing a fixed object, entering or leaving frame, or a cut.

Set it on three axes: fast against slow, regular against irregular, continuous against fragmented. Regular and slow is calm. Irregular and fast is alarm.

## Practice, not theory

The last chapter's whole argument: none of this does anything until you shoot with it. You will photograph space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, and rhythm whether or not you thought about them, and they will communicate mood and meaning to the audience either way. The only choice on offer is whether the message is yours.
